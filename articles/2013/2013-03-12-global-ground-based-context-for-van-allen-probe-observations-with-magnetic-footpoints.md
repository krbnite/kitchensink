What is the cause of ultra low-frequency (ULF) waves in the inner magnetosphere? This question is of interest to theorists and experimentalists alike. The Van Allen Probes give researchers a unique opportunity to investigate the particle populations and wave activity of the inner magnetosphere in situ, but like any airborne instrumentation, measurements are subject to spatiotemporal ambiguities. 

One instrument on the Van Allen Probes is the Radiation Belt Storm Probes Ion Composition Experiment (RBSPICE). [RBSPICE](https://rbspice.ftecs.com/Home.html) measures the fluxes of energetic ions and electrons in the inner magnetosphere. The instrument has a high time resolution, but it is limited to a single point in space at any given time.  Ground-based measurements of magnetospheric activity can help give RBSPICE data an observational context. Using available software and geomagnetic field models (e.g., one of the [Tsyganenko magnetosphere models](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/models/Tsyganenko%20Magnetic%20Field~TS05/)), one can easily identify "magnetic conjunctions" between the spacecraft and the ground (a mangetic conjunction is when two different points space or the upper atmosphere sitting on the same geomagnetic field line), and so can convert orbital trajectories of the Van Allen Probes spacecraft into "magnetic footpoint trajectories." Not only can this be done for post-event analyses, but due to the deterministic nature of the spacecraft orbits, these so-called footpoint trajectories can be mapped into the future, providing researchers the ability to determine magnetic conjunctions between the VAP spacecraft and magnetometers from all over the world for the entire duration of the mission lifetime. Using a web service such as [INTERMAGNET](https://www.intermagnet.org/), the geomagnetic data from many such magnetometers from across the globe can be accessed in near-real time, also allowing it users to access historical data.

With the ability to locate magnetic conjunctions between the VAP spacecraft and ground-based magnetometers, it is then possible to identify ULF signatures in the RBSPICE data, compare with the ground-based records, and subsequently track via other available ground stations. Such comparisons can potentially allow researchers to study radial diffusion (the outward or inward movement of trapped plasma particles) in the inner and outer magnetosphere simultaneously.

When a plasma injection takes place (the penetration of magnetotail plasma into Earth's inner magnetosphere), we are interested in what is the ULF source function (via kinetic/ion interaction) and what are the various scale sizes involved. We also want to investigate the associated spatial/temporal growth rates, how the ULF wave evolves into a "global wave," and what is the nature of nonlinear ULF dissipation (the rate at which ULF waves lose energy). 

To give context to our curiosity, consider planetary waves and gravity waves in the lower and middle atmosphere. Planetary waves are the result of the interaction of the Earth's rotation with the latitudinal variation of the Coriolis effect. The Coriolis effect is a result of the Earth's rotation, and it is responsible for the deflection of moving objects (like air masses) to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. The latitudinal variation of this effect leads to the formation of large-scale waves in the atmosphere, known as planetary waves. These waves can have significant impacts on weather patterns and climate. Gravity waves, on the other hand, are oscillations in the atmosphere that occur due to the restoring force of gravity acting on displaced air parcels. They can be generated by various sources, such as topography (mountains), convection, and wind shear. Gravity waves can propagate vertically and horizontally, transferring energy and momentum through the atmosphere. Planetary and gravity waves can interact with each other, leading to complex atmospheric dynamics. The study of these waves is important for understanding weather and climate processes, as well as for improving weather forecasting models.

This picture of gravity waves versus planetary waves in lower and middle atmosphere gives motivation for understanding the relationship between ULF waves and smaller scale phenomena in the ionosphere/magnetosphere system. In the magnetosphere, the "planetary waves" are the ULF waves, and the "gravity waves" are the smaller scale phenomena. The ULF waves can be thought of as large-scale oscillations in the magnetosphere, while the smaller scale phenomena can include various plasma instabilities, wave-particle interactions, and other localized processes. ULF waves can be generated by various sources, including solar wind interactions, geomagnetic storms, and plasma instabilities. These waves can have different frequencies and wavelengths, and they can interact with charged particles in the magnetosphere, leading to phenomena such as particle acceleration and transport. Understanding how these two types of waves interact and influence each other is crucial for gaining insights into the dynamics of the magnetosphere and, ultimately, for predicting space weather events and their potential impacts on satellite operations, communication systems, and power grids on Earth.

A motivational event occurred between 0515-0545 UT, December 2, 2012. Clearly distinguished ULF activity (with period between 6-9 minutes) was recorded by the Van Allen Probes. The seemingly innocuous [Dst activity](https://wdc.kugi.kyoto-u.ac.jp/dst_realtime/presentmonth/) during this time is typical of geomagnetic storm activity driven by high speed solar wind streams associated with corotating interaction regions (CIRs) as they propagate past Earth. Although the Dst drop isn't nearly as drastic as that typified by storms driven by coronal mass ejections (plasma blobs ejected from the Sun), for those interested in ULF activity such CIR-driven storms are very interesting specimens. 

The peculiar feature of the ULF activity recorded by RBSPICE is the cascade of energy from longer to shorter periods (this of this kind of energy cascade like when a big wave hits the beach and breaks into smaller waves). What drives this cascade of magnetic energy? How quickly does it evolve and to where does it go? How does it interact with the trapped plasma populations in the inner magnetosphere?It is questions such as these that a world-wide network of magnetometers can help answer by providing a global context to the RBSPICE observations.

In the past, my group at the [Center for Solar-Terrestrial Research](https://research.njit.edu/cstr/) at NJIT have used magnetometers stationed at Jenny Jump State Forest, NJ, and Rothera, Antarctica, to look at toroidal ULF waves (specifically Pc5 waves) driven by CIRs. The L-value at Jenny Jump is 2.5--that is, the outer boundary of the inner radiation belt, magnetically speaking. To compute power spectral density (PSD) estimates, our technique is to implement the [multitaper method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multitaper) a discrete prolate spheroidal sequence (DPSS) and the [maximum entropy method (MEM)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_entropy_spectral_estimation) for cross validation. The DPSS is a set of orthogonal sequences that are used to estimate the power spectral density of a signal. To compute the PSD using the multitaper method, we apply multiple DPSS tapers to the signal and average the resulting spectral estimates. The MEM is a method for estimating the power spectral density that maximizes the entropy of the estimated spectrum, subject to certain constraints. By using both methods, we can obtain more reliable estimates of the power spectral density and identify the presence of ULF waves in the magnetometer data.

In the December 2 event, we analyzed data from many more magnetometers, finding that such PSD estimates show Pc5 energy in the poloidal magnetic field component only. Furthermore, the wave power is found at some ground stations, but not others. It is our goal to figure out why.
